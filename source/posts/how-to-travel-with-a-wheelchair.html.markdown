As a wheelchair user, flying can be an extremely unpleasant experience. Passengers report being unable to use the toilet, being left waiting for hours to be ‘helped’ off the plane, or expensive or irreplaceable equipment being damaged by baggage handlers.



But is train travel any better? There are just as many nightmare stories from wheelchair users being unable to access toilet facilities, being left without assistance, and even resorting to crawling off trains.



The challenge of travelling without flying can be more complicated for wheelchair users, though there are some good provisions out there. Here is some information that can help those travelling with a wheelchair flight-free.



**Eurostar**



For many people, a flight-free trip to the continent will start with the Eurostar. Eurostar provisions are generally very good for wheelchair users, with a dedicated team, good facilities, and affordable pricing.



There are two wheelchair spaces per train, which are in the Eurostar Plus/Eurostar Premier area, as there’s more space here for wheelchairs and the accessible toilet. This gives lots of advantages: more space, right next to the accessible toilet, food is included, and the crew is in the same carriage, which can help if you need assistance. 



The price is £39 on all trains, no matter when you book (as long as there’s space). Your companion can also travel for £39 (which isn’t the case when you fly). If you’re travelling in a large group, contact Eurostar as they might be able to arrange for all of your companions to travel in the Eurostar Plus/Premier carriage – though they might have to pay extra for it. If you are travelling with children, just let the Eurostar team know and they’ll make sure your seats are booked together. 



Book your ticket at Eurostar.com. Choose your route and date in the planner, then select ‘wheelchair user’ from the drop-down menu. You can book for your companion at the same time. 



NB: once you’re booked your ticket, you need to book assistance separately. Eurostar recommends doing this at least 24 hours before travelling. Go to <https://www.eurostar.com/uk-en/travel-info/travel-planning/accessibility/how-to-book-assistance> You can turn up on the day, and about 20% of people do this, and they can help, although it’s obviously better if you can plan in advance.



There’s a specialist team in-house at St Pancras station that will help you. 



Specifications for dimensions and weight of wheelchair: 70cmx120cmx250g (including weight of traveller). If you need to travel with extra equipment such as a hoist and walker and oxygen tank, you can enquire with the team and they’ll let you know what they can accommodate. There’s no charge for extra equipment, but there’s a weight limit because of manual handling.



**Onwards travel**



Eurostar is the easy bit. What if you need to continue your journey on another train? 



You need to book assistance separately, again, 24 hours before travelling. Here are the contact details for each of the neighbouring countries:

* France <https://www.garesetconnexions.sncf/en/assistances-psh-pmr> 
* Belgium <https://www.belgiantrain.be/en/travel-info/prepare-for-your-journey/assistance-reduced-mobility> 
* Netherlands <https://www.nsinternational.com/en/travel-information/before-you-go/passengers-with-impaired-mobility> (international) or <https://www.ns.nl/en/travel/traveling-with-a-disability> (domestic)
* Germany <https://int.bahn.de/en/booking-information/accessible-travel> 
* Switzerland <https://www.tgv-lyria.com/fr/en/fares-promotions/fares/train-ticket-prices/specific-fares/travel-for-persons-with-reduced-mobility> 
* Italy <https://www.trenitalia.com/en/information/passengers-with-disabilities-or-reduced-mobility.html> 
* Spain <https://www.renfe.com/es/en/travel/informacion-util/servicio-de-asistencia/what-is-it> 



The difficult part can be changing stations in Paris. If you are travelling to cities in the south of France (e.g. Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux, Nice), you can avoid Paris by changing in Lille. Lille Europe is a modern station and as such has excellent step-free facilities, with multiple lifts and a level concourse. 



If you are heading eastwards into Europe, go via Brussels. Brussels Midi/Zuid is a bit harder simply because it’s so big and busy. But there are lots of lifts. 



Some metro lines and stations are wheelchair accessible, but not at Gare du Nord, unless you call in advance and book assistance, and not Gare de Lyon, so that doesn’t help exactly. 



Better option is to take the bus: all bus routes in Paris are wheelchair accessible. From Gare du Nord, the number 91 bus will take you to Gare de Lyon and Gare d’Austerlitz, number 39 goes to Gare Montparnasse, and for Saint-Lazare it’s number 26. 



Gare du Nord: check lift status here: <https://www.garesetconnexions.sncf/en/stations-services/paris-gare-du-nord/accessibility> 



to Gare de L’est it’s all step-free, with a lift connecting the upper and lower levels on Rue d’Alsace. 



Beyond that, anything is possible, and experiences are mixed. Search for assistance booking in the country you’re heading to, and check provisions at stations before you travel. 



**Travelling with other needs**



Accessibility doesn’t just involve wheelchairs, and Eurostar can help assist passengers with a wide range of needs. Details of who Eurostar can help, and what help they can provide, is here: <https://www.eurostar.com/uk-en/travel-info/travel-planning/accessibility/who-we-can-help>



This includes auditory/communication impairment, sensory, psychological or intellectual impairment, mobility impairment, non-visible disabilities, older customers, pregnant customers, visual impairment and other accessible travel requirements.
