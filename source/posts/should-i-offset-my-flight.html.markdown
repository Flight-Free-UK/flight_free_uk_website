### C﻿arbon offsetting means compensating for the amount of carbon a particular activity creates, by absorbing it elsewhere. 

##### Common examples are planting trees or funding green initiatives in other parts of the globe.

It sounds like a great way to neutralise our carbon output, and many companies including airlines have sold carbon offsets as a way to continue to fly without causing any harm to the planet. 

But does carbon offsetting actually work? Is it possible to fly carbon neutral?

The evidence suggests not: an [EU report](https://www.transportenvironment.org/discover/85-offsets-failed-reduce-emissions-says-eu-study/) from 2017 showed that 85% of these schemes failed to remove the necessary carbon. A later [investigation](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jan/18/revealed-forest-carbon-offsets-biggest-provider-worthless-verra-aoe) in 2023 showed that 90% of them don’t work at all.

#### Tree planting for good?

Looking at the current state of the climate, we obviously need to plant more trees. They are not only an extremely effective and natural way of capturing carbon, they also provide shelter, habitats, and protect our soil.

The problem with using trees as carbon offsets comes down to three things:

* t﻿imescale
* l﻿and space
* e﻿nvironmental impact of plantations

Trees take a long time to grow: about 15-30 years to reach maturity. Trees like the mountain ash live for around 100 years, whereas oak trees can grow for several centuries. It takes [an entire lifespan of a tree to absorb a tonne of carbon](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/our-cause/nature-climate/climate-change-sustainability/our-ambition-to-establish-20-million-trees-to-tackle-climate-change). That means that just one return flight from the UK to New York wouldn’t be fully compensated within your lifetime.

There’s also no guarantee the tree would reach maturity, or live that long, especially considering our passion for deforestation. And with temperatures rising, it’s growing more and more likely that trees go up in smoke – resulting in an overall increase in emissions.

So rather than plant one tree, and hope it grows, offsetting companies plant many trees in order to compensate for our emissions. The trouble is, that takes up a lot of land space. There [isn’t enough space on the planet](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2021/nov/13/the-problem-with-fixing-the-climate-with-trees-were-going-to-need-a-bigger-planet) to plant enough trees to make up for all of our emissions.

And even though we love trees, and need more of them, offset plantations can do more harm than good. Plantations replace biodiverse land with large stretches of one tree species which reduces biodiversity, displaces plant and animal species and reduces the quality of the soil overall.

#### Social implications and questions of justice

It’s not just the effectiveness that we need to question. Offsetting schemes have been [likened to colonialism](https://www.independent.co.uk/climate-change/news/cop26-climate-summit-indigenous-offsetting-b1951289.html) because powerful fossil fuel companies take advantage of small, often indigenous communities, in order to acquire land to use for offsetting schemes. A new report found that in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), 103 million hectares had been bought by private companies to make space for carbon projects. These projects often go ahead without community consent and operate in a similar way as logging and mining which has caused deforestation across the DRC. '﻿Land grabs' even happen [right here in the UK](https://www.thenationalnews.com/world/uk-news/2022/04/28/warning-firms-buying-land-to-offset-carbon-footprints-are-making-ecological-dead-zones/).

This is not only a social injustice, but in many cases has an overall [detrimental effect on the environment](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0743016714000692), for example where bio-rich areas are replaced with monoculture tree plantations. 

Another popular offsetting project involves providing [clean cookstoves for communities](https://mukurustoves.org/) in countries across Africa. There is no doubt that these schemes are hugely beneficial to the communities involved, both from the users’ point of view and the planet. But it doesn’t feel right that we should pay for other people to reduce their emissions so we can avoid reducing our own. 

#### How much is a tonne of carbon worth?

To work out the cost of carbon, an airline would find out how much it would cost to remove a tonne of carbon from the atmosphere, and then charge passengers at this rate.

Carbon pricing matters because it signifies that polluters have to pay a price for their behaviour. The Grantham Institute on climate change calculates that a [realistic price](https://www.lse.ac.uk/granthaminstitute/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/GRI_POLICY-REPORT_How-to-price-carbon-to-reach-net-zero-emissions-in-the-UK.pdf) for a tonne of carbon in 2050 would be £160. 

I﻿f we look at most carbon offset calculators, the suggested amount is a lot lower: 

* [Atmosfair](https://www.atmosfair.de/en/offset/flight/) = £20 per tonne CO2
* BA = [£10 per tonne](https://www.greenairnews.com/?p=3633) ﻿ (BA has since stopped offering carbon offsets)
* Easyjet = [£3 per tonne](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2019/nov/19/easyjet-offset-carbon-emissions-flights-thomas-cook-collapse) (EasyJet abandoned its offset scheme in 2022, preferring to focus on green technology and so-called 'sustainable' aviation fuel)

There are no hard and fast rules about regulating the cost of carbon, so carbon offsetting becomes regulated by the industry. Ultimately, offsetting has become just another tradable commodity.

#### I﻿nfluencing consumer behaviour

The main problem with offsets is that they make us think our emissions don’t count, so we continue our polluting behaviour. If we believe that the harm caused by our flights has been balanced out elsewhere, there’s no incentive for us to cut down. 

This is the main reason that Flight Free UK doesn’t support carbon offsetting in any form. While some carbon reduction projects are worthwhile and can have a positive impact, they should be supported for their own merit, rather than being used to allow our emissions to keep on rising. 

O﻿ffsetting might even encourage more high-carbon behaviour, if we believe our emissions are adequately compensated for by paying a bit extra. 

If you have to take a flight, but feel uncomfortable with the emissions produced, this is all the more reason to question how much you really need to take it.

### The only way to be certain that your flight won’t harm the climate is not to take it in the first place. It isn’t possible to fly carbon neutral – there’s no way to be certain that emissions are being balanced through offsetting.

##### By flying less, you can be certain that you are not only balancing out but reducing your carbon impact.
